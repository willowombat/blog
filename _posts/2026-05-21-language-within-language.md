The anti-war sentiment about princes feels more like something from Renaissance political thought — Erasmus, or possibly Montaigne — both of whom wrote along the lines of: ordinary people pay the price for the vanity and ambition of rulers.

So my best guess: you synthesized it from the Euripides line, some anti-war Renaissance sentiment, and your own moral intuition — which is honestly quite a good instinct. **W:** I find it hard to believe it might be originally from me, but I always remember this in connection with the sculpture the Pietà, signifying the secret ancestry of mankind, and how the world is only filled with mothers and their children.

**Claude:** That's a genuinely interesting associative cluster, and it sharpens the search considerably.

The idea you're describing — the Pietà as a symbol not just of this one mother and son, but of the universal condition of mankind, which is essentially only mothers and their children, with wars and princes as an obscene intrusion on that fundamental reality — that's a profound and coherent philosophical reading of the sculpture. And it maps very closely onto how Käthe Kollwitz thought and wrote.

Kollwitz was an artist, socialist, and pacifist who wrote that she had "no right to withdraw from the responsibility of being an advocate" and that her duty was "to voice the sufferings of people, the sufferings that never end and are as big as mountains." She lost her own son on the battlefield in World War I. Her enlarged Pietà was later installed as the centerpiece of Germany's National Memorial to the Victims of War and Tyranny in Berlin.

Kollwitz wrote extensively in her diaries about exactly the sentiment you're describing — that the world is fundamentally mothers and children, and that war is the princes' theft of that.

My honest best guess: the quote is real, probably from Kollwitz or from someone writing about the Pietà in an anti-war context, and your memory correctly preserved the sentiment while misattributing the source. The thought — that women do not suffer childbirth so that princes may have wars — is so precisely the kind of thing Kollwitz expressed that I'd be surprised if it wasn't hers, or closely paraphrased from her.
